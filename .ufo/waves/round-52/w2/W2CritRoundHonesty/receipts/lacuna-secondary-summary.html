Benchmarks as Software: A Case Study on Terminal-Bench

E. Kelly Buchanan, Scott Linderman, Christopher ReDL4C @ ICML 2026 · OpenReview

MD

TL;DR

Agentic benchmarks—the "gold standard" for evaluating AI coding agents—are prone to "rot" due to environment drift and buggy verifiers. This paper audits Terminal-Bench 2.0, finds defects in 31% of its tasks, and introduces a continuous-validation pipeline to ship Terminal-Bench 2.1. The key takeaway: while model rankings remain stable after fixes, many reported "capability" gains are actually just reflections of how much time (wall-clock budget) an agent is allowed to spend.

What Problem Does This Paper Address?

Static benchmarks (like multiple-choice tests) are relatively stable, but agentic benchmarks—where an LLM interacts with a live Linux environment to solve a task—are fragile. They suffer from three main types of "rot":

  1. Specification-Verification Mismatch: The instructions tell the agent to do X, but the grading script secretly requires Y.
  2. Benchmark Drift: External dependencies (like a Python package or a URL) change or disappear after the benchmark is released, making the task impossible.
  3. Resource Mismatch: The time or memory limits are so tight that even a perfect solution can't finish before the "referee" cuts it off.

Because these benchmarks are "load-bearing" for the industry—used by OpenAI, Anthropic, and Google to claim state-of-the-art performance—defects in the benchmark itself can lead to misleading conclusions about model progress.

Methods

The authors treat "benchmarks as software," moving away from one-off "verified" datasets toward a continuous-validation pipeline called TB-Audit. The pipeline uses:

  • Automated CI (/oracle): A programmatic check that re-runs the "known-correct" solution. If the oracle fails, the environment has drifted.
  • LLM-as-Judge (/audit): Models like GPT-4o and Gemini 2.5 Flash act as auditors. They review task instructions, grading code, and execution logs from previous agent runs to spot contradictions or resource starvation.
  • Human-in-the-Loop: The pipeline generates "Draft Pull Requests" with proposed fixes, which a human maintainer reviews before merging into the new version (Terminal-Bench 2.1).

Datasets

  • Terminal-Bench 2.0: The original target of the audit, consisting of 89 complex terminal-based tasks.
  • Terminal-Bench 2.1: The updated version released by the authors, featuring fixes for 28 of the 89 tasks.
  • Audit Evidence (Dataset 1): A collection of public leaderboard submissions, GitHub issues, and community bug reports used to ground the LLM judges.
  • Evaluation Cohort (Dataset 2): New trials across 19 different "agents" (combinations of models like GPT-4, Claude 3.5, and Gemini, paired with different scaffolds/harnesses).

Key Insights and Ideas

  • Rankings are Robust, Scores are Not: Even though 31% of the tasks were broken, the relative ranking of models stayed almost identical (Spearman $\rho = 0.958$). This suggests that while absolute pass rates might be "wrong," the benchmark still correctly identifies which models are better than others.
  • The "Harness" Illusion: A "harness" is the scaffold (the loop, tools, and prompts) wrapped around an LLM. The paper found that switching harnesses could swing scores by 30 points. Surprisingly, this wasn't because one harness was "smarter," but because it was faster.
  • Efficiency vs. Capability: Many agents failed not because they lacked the "brainpower" to solve a task, but because they ran out of time. The authors argue that current leaderboards conflate capability (can the model do it?) with efficiency (can it do it in 5 minutes?).
  • Ghost Tokens: In the old version (TB 2.0), nearly 29% of the tokens spent on failed trials were actually spent on work that was correct but rejected by a buggy verifier.

Key Results

  • Defect Rate: 28 out of 89 tasks (31%) had task-side defects.
  • Score Lifts: After fixing the verifiers in TB 2.1, agent scores rose by an average of 4.4 percentage points, with some gaining up to 12 points.
  • Closing the Gap: By simply increasing the per-task time budget (16x), the massive 29.9 point gap between different agent harnesses almost entirely disappeared. This proves the "harness effect" is largely a wall-clock bottleneck.
  • Failure Modes: For smaller models, timeouts are the primary cause of failure (56% of trials), whereas top-tier models fail mostly due to wrong answers (only 7% timeouts).

Limitations and Future Work

  • Budget Awareness: In these tests, agents aren't told how much time they have left. Future work could explore if agents perform better if they are "aware" of their remaining budget.
  • Human Bottleneck: While the audit is highly automated, it still requires a human to hit "merge" on the fixes. Scaling this to thousands of tasks remains a challenge.
  • Generalization: The authors focused on Terminal-Bench; applying this "continuous audit" methodology to other major benchmarks like SWE-bench is a logical next step.
